Claim 3 is the unattributed-roundup pattern, described rather than linked — the whole finding is that no publisher can be identified, so there is nothing to cite. **This module runs on live web pages.** Re-open every link at record time, audit what your run actually returns, and replace this file with your own output and access dates.

## The delegation brief handed to the agent

> **Q:** competitive landscape + market trends for the water bottle market (premium reusable brands plus smart/hydration-tracking entrants as one segment), to inform Vessl's launch positioning and pricing.
> **Scope:** the key players — for each: positioning, target, rough pricing, app/subscription model, standout features, obvious gaps. Plus category trends in hydration, wearables and health tracking.
> **Output:** short brief; a source (link + the source's own date if shown + date retrieved) for every meaningful claim; name **who is asserting** each claim, and keep a brand's own word separate from an independent one.
> **Guardrails:** prefer credible and current sources; flag anything you can't verify; don't pad; stop when the scope is covered.

## Source-quality audit of three claims

The verdict follows the rule in the checklist: **Trust** = all four checks pass (real, credible for the claim — primary or clearly attributed, current as of the date recorded, supported when you open it) — safe to cite as a fact; **Verify-first** = mixed checks — usable as directional, confirm against a primary source or re-word it before citing it as a number; **Drop** = two or more checks fail, or no source can be identified.

| Claim | Cited source | Accessed | Credible? | Verified against source? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. LARQ positions PureVis on self-cleaning UV-C purification, not intake tracking | LARQ's own PureVis support page | 2026-09-04 | Yes — primary, for a claim about how it frames its own product | Yes — the page says it, in those words | **Trust** |
| 2. "HidrateSpark's app is free — no subscription" | HidrateSpark's own app page + the iOS App Store listing | 2026-09-04 | Yes — primary, plus the platform's own listing | Partly — free to download, but PREMIUM is a paid subscription | **Verify-first** |
| 3. "Smart bottles ≈ 15% of premium bottle sales" | "Best Smart Water Bottles of 2026" roundup — no author, no publisher | 2026-09-04 | No — not credible for a market-share figure | No — the page attributes it to "industry data" and nothing else | **Drop** |

**1 — trust it, worded as what it proves.** The support page opens, it's LARQ's own, it's current as of the date recorded, and it says what the brief says: PureVis cleans the water and the bottle at the touch of a button, eliminating "up to 99%\* of bio-contaminants such as E. coli," with no intake tracking mentioned in the product FAQ. All four checks pass → **Trust.** The discipline is in the wording. This supports *"LARQ positions the PureVis on water quality, not hydration tracking"* — a fact about the competitive landscape, which is what the CMO asked for. It does not support *"LARQ's bottle eliminates 99% of bio-contaminants"*; that's the brand's own claim about its own product, carrying its own asterisk. A primary source is credible for what a company asserts, not for whether the assertion is independently proven.

**2 — real source, wrong sentence.** This is the one that should worry you, because nothing about it looks weak: the citation is primary, the pages open, both are current. Three checks pass. **Supported** is where it breaks. The App Store listing reads "Free · In-App Purchases" and lists HidrateSpark PREMIUM among them ($1.99 and $14.99 on the listing), described as a monthly and annual subscription with a one-month free trial; the brand's own app page describes features gated behind "a PREMIUM subscription." The download is free. The product is not subscription-free. One check down → **Verify-first: re-word to "free app with an optional paid Premium tier" and confirm the current tier pricing before it informs the pricing call.** The agent didn't hallucinate — it compressed a two-part fact into a one-part claim, and the compression happens to be the part the CMO is making a decision on. Checking the source *list* would have passed this. Only opening the page and reading it against the brief's exact wording catches it.

**3 — the most citable-looking line, and the one that has to go.** It reads like the strongest claim in the brief: a year-stamped title, clean comparison tables, a figure stated to the point. Opening it shows what the formatting was hiding — no author byline, no masthead, no publisher or "about" page, the same paragraphs running verbatim across sibling review domains, and the 15% attributed to "industry data suggests" with nothing behind it. **Real** passes: the page exists. **Credible** fails — an unattributed aggregator is not a source for a market-share figure. **Supported** fails too, in the only sense that matters: the page doesn't evidence the number, it repeats it. Two checks down → **Drop.** Note what that does and doesn't mean. Smart bottles plainly are some share of premium sales, and this audit says nothing about what that share is. It says there is no source here, so you can't put a number on it yet. Re-source it to a named firm that publishes the segment split, or cut it — don't soften the wording and keep it.

**Marked-up verdict:** ship claim 1, worded as how LARQ positions itself. Re-word claim 2 to "free app, paid Premium tier" and re-check the tier pricing before it reaches a pricing discussion — as written it would have understated a competitor's monetization. Strike claim 3 outright; there's nothing behind it to fix. A confident brief is a starting point, not a source of truth.
