# Worked Demo — Anatomy of a Goal-Rich Brief

*The real content: the finished walkthrough the demo produces. This is the reference version of what the instructor demonstrates.*

## The starting point (the naked prompt)

The marketer wants a re-engagement email to free users who haven't upgraded and types the prompt most people would:

> "Write an email to get free users to upgrade to paid."

The output is competent and completely generic — it could belong to any product, any audience, any moment. That is the point: a generic prompt forces the model to guess at audience, goal, and constraints, and it guesses generically.

## The brief scaffold

A goal-rich brief front-loads five decisions the marketer already owns:

- **Audience** — who exactly this is for
- **Goal** — the one action we want
- **Context** — the moment and the facts the model can't guess
- **Constraints** — what we can and can't say
- **Success criteria** — what "good" looks like

## The rewrite (worked)

- **Audience:** free users active in the last 30 days who have never hit the paywall
- **Goal:** drive a click to the pricing page — not brand warmth
- **Context:** they already get value from the free tier; this is a value message, not a first-touch intro
- **Constraints:** no discounting; this is a value message, not a promo
- **Success criteria:** the email earns a click to pricing, not just an open

Running this brief produces an email that speaks to a specific person at a specific moment.

## What changed, and why it matters

- The CTA got sharper — a real next step instead of "learn more."
- The value proposition stopped being generic and spoke to a real moment.
- The message addressed people who already know the product, instead of re-explaining it.

The brief isn't longer for its own sake. Every added line removed an assumption the model would otherwise have made wrong.

## The failure-mode frame (sets up the exercise)

Prompts go wrong in a few distinct ways:

- **Over-prescribed** — too many rigid rules; the fix is usually to *remove* detail.
- **Under-specified** — missing the key facts.
- **Vague** — no real goal at all.

A goal-rich brief fixes all three. Naming the failure type is what points you to the fix.

## Key takeaway

The model isn't the bottleneck — the brief is. A goal-rich brief is just the marketer doing the thinking upfront instead of outsourcing it to a guess.
