# Worked Demo — Score One Tool Against the Framework

*The real content: the finished walkthrough the demo produces.*

## Why a framework at all

Most tool decisions are made on demo dazzle, not fit. The framework forces the boring questions that actually predict success. Its dimensions:

- **Capability fit** — does it do the specific job well and dependably, on your real task?
- **Fit with your stack** — does it connect to what you already run, or is it copy-paste?
- **Cost vs. value** — is the price justified against a baseline, not against zero?
- **Data & privacy** — where does your data go; is it used to train their models?
- **Learning curve / adoption** — can you and your team actually roll it out?
- **Brand-voice control / brand-safety** — can you keep output on-brand and compliant? This is the marketing-specific dimension generic checklists miss.

## Scoring one tool (worked)

Take a single AI copywriting tool and score it dimension by dimension, narrating the reasoning:

- **Capability:** strong — copy comes out sharp and on-brand once trained on samples.
- **Integration:** weak — no native CMS connection; every draft is copy-paste.
- **Data/privacy:** check whether inputs train their model; assume you must verify the terms.
- **Brand-voice control:** can you lock tone, or does it drift off-brand across a batch?

A tool can score high on capability and still be the wrong choice if it fails on integration or data privacy. The scorecard makes those tradeoffs visible instead of letting the demo hide them.

## Stress-test a score

Ask Claude: *"Am I being too generous on integration given we'd have to copy-paste every draft?"* Use it to challenge the soft scores, not just to total them.

## Build-vs-buy (the beat that matters most)

Run the same copywriting task through a general-purpose LLM with a good brief. Compare it to the specialized tool's output. The most overlooked option is the general model you already have — every specialized tool has to earn its price against that near-free baseline.

## The verdict

Total the score and state one call — **adopt, pilot, or pass** — with the reason. The value isn't the number; it's the documented reasoning you can hand to a skeptical manager.

## Key takeaway

A structured evaluation converts an impulse into a recommendation. The scorecard is what lets you say no to a shiny tool and yes to the right one, with reasons — and the honest first question is always whether a good brief and the LLM you already pay for would do the job.
