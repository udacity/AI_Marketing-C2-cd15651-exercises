# Worked Demo — Score One Tool Against the Framework

*The real content: the finished walkthrough the demo produces.*

## Why a framework at all

Most tool decisions are made on demo dazzle, not fit. The framework forces the boring questions that actually predict success. Its dimensions:

- **Capability fit** — does it do the specific job well and dependably, on your real task?
- **Fit with your stack** — does it connect to what you already run, or is it copy-paste?
- **Cost vs. value** — is the price justified against a baseline, not against zero?
- **Data & privacy** — where does your data go; is it used to train their models?
- **Learning curve / adoption** — can you and your team actually roll it out?
- **Brand-voice control / brand-safety** — can you keep output on-brand and compliant? This is the marketing-specific dimension generic checklists miss.

## The tool on the table

**Framecast**, an AI ad-creative adaptation tool. You give it a master creative and it produces every placement size you need — feed square, story vertical, in-stream, display banner — recropping, reflowing the type and repositioning the logo for each frame.

The real task, not the vendor's: our monthly paid-social refresh. **40 master creatives × 14 placement specs = 560 exports**, done today by a contract designer over about a week. Score the tool on that job.

## Scoring one tool (worked)

Go dimension by dimension and narrate the reasoning out loud:

- **Capability fit:** run the actual 560. Twice. Every export came back at spec, safe zones respected, logo never clipped, no failures — and the second run matched the first. That's the whole dimension: does it do the job, and does it do it *dependably*.
- **Fit with your stack:** it pulls masters straight from our asset library and pushes finished sizes into two of the three ad platforms we run. The third is still a manual export. Real integration, with a gap.
- **Cost vs. value:** price it against the baseline, never against zero. The baseline here is the contractor week we already pay for — and against that the flat team rate is a fraction of the cost.
- **Data & privacy:** where do the uploads live, and what may the vendor do with them? Read the terms before you score this, not after.
- **Brand-voice control / brand-safety:** for a creative tool this is about whether the output still looks like us and still says something we can stand behind. The brand kit locks the fonts, colours and logo clear-space. Two things it doesn't lock: which part of the image survives a vertical crop, and whether a reflowed legal disclaimer is still legible.

Note where that last observation *lands*. "It cropped the product out of frame on some verticals" is not a mechanical failure — every file hit its spec. It's a brand judgment, so it scores under brand-voice, not capability. Deciding which dimension owns a finding is half the work.

## Stress-test a score

Don't just total the scores — argue with them. Ask Claude: *"I gave data & privacy a 2 because they can train on our uploaded creative. Is that enough to pass on the tool?"*

The pushback is useful: the 2 is the right score, but it isn't load-bearing here. These are ads about to be published to the entire internet, so training exposure costs us very little. It *would* be decisive the day we put unreleased product shots through it. A low score is a fact; whether it decides the call is a separate question, and stating that separation is what makes the memo defensible.

## Build-vs-buy (the beat that matters most)

Before you buy anything, try to do the job with what you already have. So: the same masters, the same 14 placement specs, through the general models and image tooling already on our bill.

It gets close on one frame and falls apart at volume — it re-renders type instead of preserving the brand font, drifts the logo position, and won't hold exact pixel dimensions reliably. Checking and fixing 560 exports by hand costs more than the contractor does.

Here, the baseline loses — and it had to be tested to know that. Sometimes it clears the bar and you buy nothing at all. The point isn't which way it goes; it's that the near-free option you already own is the bar every paid tool has to clear, and you find out by running it, not by assuming.

## Size the decision

Match rigour to stakes. This one is not quite a two-way door: the brand kit and all 14 placement specs get built inside Framecast, and the monthly workflow reroutes around it. Cancelling means rebuilding that setup and going back to contractor weeks. That's why the privacy score got argued with instead of waved through. On durability, the vendor is five years old and profitable, so longevity isn't the blocker.

## The verdict

Total it, then state one call — **adopt, pilot, or pass** — and name the tradeoff you accepted.

**Adopt, with two guardrails:** no unreleased product creative through it until the no-training tier is priced, and the disclaimer legibility plus the vertical hero crops stay a human check before publish. **Tradeoff accepted:** a 2 on data & privacy, taken knowingly because the assets are public the day after they're made.

The value isn't the number. It's the documented reasoning you can hand to a skeptical manager.

## Scaling to more options

One tool and a baseline fit in a table with the reasoning inside it. Four options don't — at that width the Reasoning column stops fitting on a page. So it moves rather than disappears: scores stay in the grid, the *why* becomes a short findings note below it (one bullet per option), and the memo carries the call. Same reasoning, relocated.

## Key takeaway

A structured evaluation converts an impulse into a recommendation. The scorecard is what lets you say no to a shiny tool and yes to the right one, with reasons — and the honest first question is always whether a good brief and the tools you already pay for would do the job.
